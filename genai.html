ABOUT THE PROJECT

A research project examining how prompting behaviour, session duration and perceived AI helpfulness relate to task completion and reuse. It combines statistical analysis of 10,000 synthetic learning sessions with a qualitative comparison of educational responses from five GenAI tools.

10,000 synthetic sessions4 research hypotheses5 GenAI tools reviewed

Simulation-based analysis. These are generated records, not observations collected from 10,000 students.

01 / RESEARCH QUESTION

Beyond access to AI: how is it being used?

The study focuses on the interaction rather than the tool alone. Does issuing multiple prompts relate to task completion? Does a longer session suggest a better outcome? And does perceived helpfulness translate into reuse?

02 / DATA & RESEARCH DESIGN

Translate broad questions into testable variables.

The paper uses AI Assistant Usage in Student Life, a synthetic Kaggle dataset. Each row represents one simulated session. The report describes no missing values, so no imputation was required.

BEHAVIOUR

Prompting

RefinedPrompt marks more than one prompt. TotalPrompts records the count; its squared term tests curvature.

ENGAGEMENT

Time & perception

Session duration and perceived AI helpfulness describe engagement from two different perspectives.

OUTCOMES

Completion & reuse

Binary completion and reuse indicators are distinct from the multi-category final task status.

Binary fields were encoded as 0/1. Four hypothesis-specific analyses followed: a chi-square independence test, quadratic logistic regression, and two linear regression analyses.

A measurement decision that matters

“Refined” means multiple prompts in this dataset. The analysis does not inspect prompt wording, so it cannot distinguish thoughtful revision from repeated or unclear requests.

Four questions. Different levels of evidence.

04 / QUALITATIVE RESPONSE REVIEW

The same educational task, different response styles.

A separate exercise compared ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Gemini, Claude and Copilot across five educational prompts. The team discussed clarity, structure, examples, tone and instructional usefulness in the sampled responses.

01

Explain a concept

Make opportunity cost understandable to a 10-year-old through an everyday example.

02

Compare ideas

Explain differences between formative and summative assessment.

03

Guide a learner

Offer step-by-step help with algebraic equations.

04

Challenge a claim

Construct a counterargument about technology and learning outcomes.

05

Give contextual advice

Advise aspiring data science students from an educator’s perspective.

This is a qualitative review of sampled answers, not a controlled benchmark or a current ranking of the five tools. The report does not document a standardized scoring rubric or model versions.

05 / IMPLICATIONS & NEXT STEPS

Turn the questions into a better study.

  1. Measure the quality of revision.

    Review prompt content for specificity, context and meaningful changes instead of relying on prompt count alone.

  2. Separate activity from learning.

    Pair usage measures with comprehension checks, task quality and later retention. More time or more prompts need not mean more learning.

  3. Test design ideas with real learners.

    Prompt-writing guidance and feedback are candidate interventions. A controlled study could test their effects rather than infer them from synthetic associations.

Study limitations & source notes

Source: How Prompt Engineering Shapes Learning Outcomes in GenAI-based Education, Final Paper Group 1. This portfolio page summarizes the academic team report and reconciles its narrative with visible appendix outputs.

Synthetic data cannot validate effects in real learners. Prior ability and task difficulty were not controlled. H1 reports no contingency counts or effect size, and its p-value is unadjusted across four exploratory hypotheses.

The appendix’s H2 quadratic coefficient is −0.0009488, differing from the rounded value in the narrative. H3 shows AI_AssistanceLevel ~ UsedAgain, whereas the narrative describes predicting reuse from satisfaction. H4 applies linear regression to a coded category; its plot uses 1–4 while the methods describe 0–3. The ordering and appropriate categorical model need review.

No data or executable analysis was supplied with the paper, so these results have not been independently reproduced. Suggested product and teaching changes remain proposals, not demonstrated outcomes.
